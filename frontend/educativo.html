01

Why does the Sun barely rise at the south pole?

The Moon’s axis is tilted only ~1.5° relative to its orbit (the Earth’s is 23.4°). That is why there are no marked seasons, and near the poles the Sun circles almost glued to the horizon. Shadows are extremely long.

02

Why does the Earth barely move in the sky?

The Moon always shows the same face to the Earth (synchronous rotation). From the near side, the Earth stays almost fixed in the sky and only wobbles because of libration. From the far side it is never seen directly. From the south pole it sits close to the horizon, and the terrain easily blocks it.

03

What does the terrain change?

The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is no refraction: the horizon is geometric and set by the terrain. With a flat horizon you see the Sun as soon as it rises above 0°; with terrain, a mountain of a few degrees is enough to block it. Here the same calculation is compared with and without terrain.

04

How long is a lunar day?

A lunar day (sunrise to sunrise) lasts ≈ 29.5 Earth days: it is the time the Moon takes to repeat its phase. It is longer than the time it takes to spin on its axis (27.3 d) because, while it spins, the Earth and the Moon also move around the Sun. A night can last about two weeks; at the south pole, with the terrain, the cycles get shorter and blend together.

05

What happens in a place that almost never sees the Sun?

At the south pole there are craters whose floor never receives direct light: the Sun always stays below the rim. These “cold traps” can drop below −200 °C and signs of water ice have been detected, which is why they are so interesting. Here the time is split into three states: Sun visible, Sun blocked by the terrain, and night (Sun below 0°).

An almost vertical axis: the Sun stays low

From the pole, the Sun’s maximum height equals the axis tilt. On the Earth that is 23.4°; on the Moon, only 1.5°. Change the tilt and watch the angle between the light ray and the local horizon (the white line).
